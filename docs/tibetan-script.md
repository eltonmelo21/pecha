# The Tibetan Script

**English** · [Português](escrita-tibetana.md) · [繁體中文](tibetan-script.zh-Hant.md)

The pechas made by this skill are written in the Tibetan script, set in Noto Serif Tibetan. This page gathers what is known about where the script came from, keeping the account of the tradition apart from what studies discuss, and shows the alphabet with its transliteration. Each statement names the work it comes from, listed at the end.

## The Traditional Account

- Tibetan histories credit the script to Thonmi Sambhota, a minister of the emperor Songtsen Gampo in the seventh century (Chhosphel 2010; van Schaik 2011, pp. 49–50).
- In the story, the emperor sent him to India as a young man. There a brahmin taught him twenty scripts, and on his return he shaped a Tibetan alphabet from them (van Schaik 2011, pp. 49–50).
- He is also credited with treatises on grammar, two of which are still foundational texts for the rules of Tibetan grammar (Chhosphel 2010).
- The earliest known sources of this account are a treasure text, the *Pillar Testament*, and a clan history, the *Testament of Ba* (van Schaik 2011, p. 49).

## What Studies Discuss

- No surviving document can be traced with certainty to Thonmi Sambhota, and his existence has been questioned, Tibetan scholars among those who question it (van Schaik 2011, pp. 51–52).
- Comparing the shapes of the letters, van Schaik finds the Late Gupta script of North India and Nepal the most likely model. He sets aside the scripts of Kashmir, Khotan and Central Asia, and finds that the Zhangzhung scripts of the Bön tradition probably appear only after the tenth century (van Schaik 2011, pp. 60–67, 75).
- The script probably took shape under Songtsen Gampo and was settled in the 630s or 640s, shortly before the laws were written down in 654–655, as the Old Tibetan Annals record (van Schaik 2011, pp. 52–53, 75–76).
- The oldest surviving dated Tibetan inscription is the Zhol pillar in Lhasa, from about 767 (van Schaik 2011, pp. 51, 56–57).
